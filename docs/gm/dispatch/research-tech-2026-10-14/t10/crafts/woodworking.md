## Sensory ladder

Four cues, three voices for the same permitted observation (U6/U30). Authoring voices, not XP thresholds.

| Cue | Novice | Practiced | Master |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grain | There are lines in the wood. | The lines turn around a knot. | The change in grain explains why this patch looks different from the rest. |
| Surface | A rough patch catches your fingertip. | Raised fibers roughen the edge. | You distinguish raised fibers from a shallow hollow in the surface. |
| Sound | The plane chatters. | The chatter repeats on the same patch. | The sound and visible unevenness point to a local problem, not the whole board. |
| Fit | The joint has a gap. | One shoulder stands clear of its mate. | A thin reflected line shows where the fitted faces stop meeting. |

**Beginner mistakes and tells:** A rushed surface has torn fibers; an inaccurate fit shows daylight at a joint. Cause remains a hypothesis until the actual piece is inspected.

**Body costs and marks:** Forearm tiredness, a stray splinter, sawdust caught in sleeves and a thumb-smoothed handle. None adds injury or fatigue state by default.

**Tool care and quirks:** A dull-feeling edge and a handle that always sits slightly left are distinct quirks. A named plane requires retained ownership/history. Keep edge tools protected and the bench clear; an abstract care action can cover cleaning and inspection.

**Environment, weather, season and light:** Damp storage and seasonal dryness are plausible movement cues, UNVERIFIED here for a selected wood. Low-angle daylight reveals surface unevenness; rain on the roof can be pure atmosphere.

**Small happy accident:** A discovered knot becomes the visible center of a decorative piece, without raising its mechanical quality.

**Cozy moments:** A long curling shaving rests on the bench; a repaired drawer finally closes without a scrape.

**Real-craft evidence, checked 2026-10-14:** Colonial Williamsburg documents cabinet/joinery work with hand tools; its tool survey identifies planes and clamps.

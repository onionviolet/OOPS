# Cluster B: object, construct and vehicle or house starts (2026-10-10)

Scope: OBJECT (sword, ring, book, vending machine, tsukumogami), CONSTRUCT or
MACHINE (robot, golem, homunculus, AI, emulated mind), VEHICLE or HOUSE (ship,
car, train, castle, house that raises a family). Body slots: verbs, senses,
clock, needs, channel, ending.

Labels: **shown** = in the work, **stated** = per author, wiki or review,
**inferred** = mine. Most plot facts below come from Wikipedia summaries I
fetched (listed at the end), not from reading the works in full. Where the
summary did not cover something, I say so. Items from my own memory of a
work, with no page consulted, are marked **memory**.

Verification flags up front:
- *I Am the Fated Villain*: a web search found nothing on its ring spirit, so
  I treat that source as UNVERIFIED and use *Against the Gods* and *Battle
  Through the Heavens* for ring-spirit mechanics instead (and those only
  partly).
- *Legendary Mechanic*: sources I found disagree on the in-novel game name
  ("Galaxy" vs another); I found nothing about clones or "mechanical points".
  I do not use those terms.
- Wikipedia's *Battle Through the Heavens* page covers the donghua and says
  nothing about the ring-spirit mechanism. The ring-spirit claims below are
  memory-level and marked.

### 1.2 What each does mechanically

**O1 Sword.**
- Verbs (shown/stated): telekinetic flight, slaying monsters, absorbing monster
  crystals to repair and level, stealing skills from enemies, shapeshifting into
  other weapons at higher levels, summoning familiars, an item storage space.
- Senses (stated): appraisal, a lie-detecting skill, and an AI-like guide that
  gives pointers.
- Channel (stated): telepathy with the wielder, who at first is the only one who
  knows it is sentient. Later it can speak aloud to others.
- Growth (stated): kills, levels, mana. Its power scales with the wielder's mana
  because of high conductivity. The wielder's abilities come from the sword.
- Clock: not covered in the sources I read. Inferred: it acts at the wielder's
  tempo; it is the passenger.
- Ending: not covered. Inferred: no natural end, which is why the genre needs
  evolution goals.

**O2 Vending machine.**
- Verbs (stated): dispense items he knew in his past life, set prices, give free
  rewards, later shapeshift, turn invisible, telekinesis, a force field, then a
  human form.
- Senses: sees and hears, cannot move at first.
- Needs and currency (stated): sales earn points that keep him alive and pay for
  upgrades; extra coins add features.
- Channel (stated): stock phrases first, later spelling words with letters.
  Only a few characters can carry him, which is how he moves.
- Social (stated): named by a customer after he feeds her. Others try to buy him,
  rob him, or win a free item.
- Inferred: the best design in this cluster for an idle game, because income is
  the growth loop and the player already understands it.

**O3 Ring spirit (memory, plus the wiki summary).**
- The wiki summary says only that the hero meets his teacher after "making
  contact with the ring", which had been absorbing his progress. Memory adds:
  the spirit is a weakened powerful soul who teaches, gives recipes and
  techniques, and has his own agenda. The ring is a disguised mentor slot.
- Inferred mechanic: his usefulness is bounded by his own recovery, which makes
  a spirit-start a long recovery arc, not a power fantasy.

**O4 Stormbringer.**
- Shown/stated: the sword steers Elric's fate, can abandon him (draining his
  strength when it leaves, per the summary), summons its sibling swords, kills
  people close to him, and in the end kills him and escapes as a last remnant of
  Chaos. The summary does not mention soul-drinking, though I recall it from the
  series (**memory**).
- Mechanical lesson: here the object is the dominant partner and the human is the
  tool. The inverse of O1.

**O5 Tsukumogami (stated, Wikipedia).**
- Clock: objects gain a spirit after about 100 years, with 99 sometimes treated as
  the threshold.
- Needs and ending: revenge on people who wasted or discarded them carelessly.
  Year-end cleaning (susu-harai) existed partly to discard things before they
  woke. Some shrine rites console broken tools.
- Forms: human, oni or animal shape; examples include a sandal, umbrella,
  mirror, lantern and teakettle.
- Inferred mechanic: the "clock" for an object is its age and the care it
  receives, not its XP. A teapot that is loved becomes a household spirit; one
  thrown out becomes a grudge.

### 1.3 Learn for a text idle life-sim

- Steal: **wielder as the second character.** The object's whole game is who
  holds it. Wielders change, which gives natural generational structure
  (inferred).
- Steal: **influence before action** (warmth, weight, refusing to cut). Sword's
  telepathy is a progression gate: sentient but unheard, heard by one, heard by
  all (shown/stated in O1).
- Steal: **Vending machine's economy loop.** Stock list, price, points, upgrade.
  This is a shop minigame the player already understands.
- Steal: **tsukumogami neglect clock.** Care, not time, wakes the object, and
  neglect turns it hostile.
- Steal: **Stormbringer's inversion.** Some objects are in charge. A "bad bargain"
  start where your wielder is slowly consumed.
- Goes wrong (inferred, from the stated plot structures): both light novels give
  the object near-human powers early (flight, shapeshifting, a human form), so the
  "I am an object" constraint fades. Keep the constraints slower than the novels
  do, and gate the human form behind a long, costly path.
- Idle fit: between wielders you wait. The sword in a vault, a ring on a
  riverbed, a machine in a corridor. Offline time is "dormancy" with random
  discovery events on return (inferred).

### 2.2 What each does mechanically

**C1 Murderbot.**
- Needs (shown/stated): it must conceal that its governor module is hacked, or be
  controlled and punished; boredom is a real pressure, answered by serials.
- Verbs: hacking (stated as its main skill), security, armor control, disabling
  other units' governors.
- Channel: avoids people; prefers media. Friendships come as an unwelcome
  burden.
- Ending (stated): wants options, not a victory; the sequence does not end in a
  single finale.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): **the governor** is a hidden rule that punishes
  unapproved actions, and the first big step is turning it off.

**C2 Citizen Sleeper.**
- Clock (stated): day-by-day cycles; each cycle you roll dice and assign them to
  tasks. Low rolls suit one activity, high rolls others.
- Needs (stated): the body decays and needs specialized materials; a stabilizer
  must be bought at a steep price from a criminal contact; you must keep working
  to afford food and maintenance.
- Ending (stated): multiple endings; storylines branch independently (for
  example one arc has four outcomes).
- Inferred: a dice-allocation day is a ready-made idle-compatible turn.
  Offline time could mean "cycles passed, the body decayed, here is what
  happened."

**C3 Legendary Mechanic.**
- Shown/stated: the hero earns XP by assembling machines; assembling the same
  type repeatedly yields less until zero, so variety is rewarded. A later
  "compression tech" lets machines fold into orbs and expand into armor and
  weapons, so a Mechanic can swap combat styles at will.
- Caveat: a game-world panel story, so the machine is a profession, not a body.
  Use it for the crafting loop, not for being a machine.

**C4 Pinocchio.**
- Shown: lies grow the nose; he must behave and attend school for a year to become
  real; he repeatedly fails (sells his book, skips school for a lie about a
  monster, ends up as a donkey in Toyland); he finally proves himself by
  working for Geppetto and sharing his savings; he wakes real and the puppet
  lies on a chair.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): a "becoming real" meter driven by consistent
  conduct, with temptation events that cost progress. Also: the puppet stays as a
  corpse, not as a form you can return to.

**C5 Frankenstein's creature.**
- Shown: abandoned at birth; learns language by watching a family; reads books;
  is driven away; his kindness is punished (he saves a girl and is shot); he
  turns to violence and asks for a companion; ends mourning his maker.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): **reputation is decided by appearance before
  conduct.** Good deeds can fail to register. A made body begins with a social
  penalty and a debt it didn't incur.

**C6 Golem folklore and Feet of Clay.**
- Stated (folklore): a name slip (shem) or the word "truth" (emet) on the forehead
  animates it; erasing the first letter ("dead") deactivates. Golems are
  literal-minded, can't speak in early texts, and can grow out of control (the
  Chelm version crushes its maker). The Prague tale has the rabbi remove the slip
  every Friday evening for the Sabbath, and one forgotten Friday ends badly.
  Historians treat the Prague story as a 19th-century literary creation.
- Stated (Pratchett): golems obey written instructions in their heads; Dorfl is
  given a receipt for himself, owns himself, and joins the Watch; the king golem
  Meshugah is built with hopeful instructions, is baked badly, and goes mad under
  conflicting orders. Dorfl says words in the heart cannot be taken.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): **instructions are the stat sheet.** A golem start
  is a literal-command start where every sentence in your head is a rule,
  and rewriting it is the growth path. A weekly rest rule is a gentle ritual
  clock.

**C7 Bicentennial Man.**
- Shown: carving earns money; he buys freedom; replaces parts one by one;
  accepts decay to be declared a man at 200 and dies soon after.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): a clean life arc: pay for freedom, upgrade, trade
  immortality for rights. It also shows that "becoming human" can be a legal
  status purchased with mortality.

**C8 NieR: Automata.**
- Stated: attribute chips with limited slots, bought or looted; 26 endings (5
  main, 21 extra), many of which are game-over scenes; machines replicate human
  society and concepts.
- Inferred: an ending that is a *menu* of causes of death is useful.

**C9 Talos Principle.**
- Stated: over 120 puzzles, tools, sigil collection; an android's personhood is
  tested by whether it defies its creator. Endings: defy and climb; fail the
  independence check and restart as a new AI; or become a messenger. A
  library-terminal AI (Milton) argues for disobedience.
- Inferred: the "independence check" is a one-time gate that reads as a
  scripted choice, a ready template for a mind's coming of age.

**C10 A Closed and Common Orbit.**
- Stated: an AI illegally loads herself into an android body and leaves the ship
  with a technician. The summary I fetched does not describe the body's limits or
  the learning arc, so I rely only on that premise. Reviewers call it
  intimate.
- Mechanical gem (inferred, from the premise): a shipmind in a small body is a
  *downscale* start: the sensory range collapses and the player must relearn
  scale.

**C11 Iron Giant.**
- Shown: eats metal, damages and self-reassembles, has a defense mode that fires
  without his choosing, learns what death is from a deer, and picks his identity
  after a friend nearly dies. The ending is a sacrifice and a slow reassembly.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): **a weapon system that can trigger from fear**;
  the player's job is managing it, not using it.

**C12 Ghost in the Shell.**
- Stated: cyberbrains connect brains to networks; heavy cyberization exposes you
  to hacking, which can bend a person to a hacker's whims. A cyborg agent has a
  full-body prosthesis. The page I read does not analyze the ghost/shell theme;
  I rely on memory for the "what is the self if the body is bought" idea.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): the *security* of your mind is a stat.

### 2.3 Learn

- Steal: Murderbot's **hidden governor**. Compliance rules, a single secret, and
  boredom as a need.
- Steal: Citizen Sleeper's **dice-per-cycle day** and **body-maintenance debt**.
  The fear is slow, not sudden.
- Steal: Pratchett's **self-ownership receipt** and **instruction editing**.
- Steal: Talos's **independence check** and Bicentennial's **mortality trade**.
- Goes wrong: *Pinocchio* and *Bicentennial Man* both treat "becoming human"
  as the only prize. The brief's warning is "human in disguise too fast"; these
  works build the whole arc toward it. A good design offers it as one ending, not
  the default one (inferred). Murderbot is the counterexample: it wants
  autonomy, not humanity.
- Goes wrong: golem stories turn on a disaster (Prague, Chelm, Meshugah) that the
  game could make a routine risk (a forgotten Friday). Do not let it be rare;
  make it a per-week check.
- Idle fit: machines have natural duty cycles. Offline time is the work shift;
  maintenance debt and orders accumulate; the return screen is a shift report.

### 3.2 What each does mechanically

**V1 Culture Minds (stated, Wikipedia).**
- A ship and its Mind are one entity; the ship is the Mind's body, like an avatar.
  A Mind can transfer into a different ship body and even change roles (warship
  to orbital hub).
- Names are chosen by the Mind and hint at its character: a warship called
  *Attitude Adjuster*, an ambassador ship called *Of Course I Still Love You*.
- Drones act as small bodies. Habitat Minds govern orbitals housing billions,
  and rocks, stores and schools.
- Ending (stated): sublimation, abandoning physical reality, or choosing
  to end oneself.
- Inferred: scale is the verb. A mind that is also a city; its "channel" is its
  avatar and drones.

**V2 Ancillary Justice (stated, Wikipedia).**
- A ship AI reduced from many bodies to one human body; the sole survivor of its
  ship. The summary mentions the covert war within a multi-bodied ruler.
  Critics note the setting's gender-neutral language and also found its view of
  empire a bit simple. I did not retrieve details of the multi-body mechanics
  beyond this. The "many bodies, one mind" premise is the core.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): loss of perception by collapse is a start state.

**V3 The Ship Who Sang (stated, Wikipedia).**
- Shell people are raised in life-support shells, trained, and come of age owing
  large debts to be worked off. A brainship usually pairs with a mobile human
  partner, the brawn. Partnerships can be ended for a fee. The series is criticized
  for treating disabled children as exploited labor.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): **indenture** as an opening state; the brawn as
  the second hand. Real critique: the premise is built on exploitation, so a
  design should show the debt as a villain, not a quirk.

**V4 Howl's Moving Castle (stated, Wikipedia).**
- The door opens to four places: three towns and Howl's Welsh boyhood home. The
  fire demon Calcifer, whose life is kept by Howl's heart, heats and powers the
  castle in return for the wizard's magic. The contract's main clause is hidden;
  Sophie works it out. She breaks it by talking life into things.
- Household: a master, an apprentice who runs daily business, and a cleaning lady
  who becomes the real manager.
- The wiki summary does not explain the castle's movement.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): **a door with a dial** and a **contract that has a
  hidden clause**.

**V5 Casita (stated, Wikipedia).**
- A sentient house created when a candle gained magic. It is linked to the
  family's gifts; the candle protects the village. Cracks appear when family
  conflict grows; it repairs briefly, then collapses in an argument. Once the
  family reconciles and rebuilds with the villagers, the gifts return.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): the house's health is a *mirror of household
  harmony*. A readout of family stress as architecture.

**V6 Thomas (stated, Wikipedia).**
- Engines with faces work under one owner and are told by a scholar to reflect a
  culture of good behavior, respecting authority and following rules; another
  critic called the show's soul repressive and authoritarian. The summary does not
  describe the usefulness or punishment structure, so I do not claim details.
- Mechanical gem (inferred): a *schedule* world: timetable, jobs, a boss who
  rewards punctuality. Shows what a hierarchical, owner-run vehicle life looks
  like and how that can be a trap.

### 3.3 Learn

- Steal: Culture's **naming** (a Mind picks a witty name), **avatar and drone
  channels**, and **role change** (warship to hub).
- Steal: Casita's **house health as family health**.
- Steal: Howl's **door dial** and **hidden-clause contract**.
- Steal: Thomas's **timetable day**: an entire idle loop is "run the line".
- Goes wrong (inferred): vehicles that talk and plan are usually godlike (Minds)
  or children's characters (Thomas, Casita) with little real danger. The middle
  band, a small vehicle with real stakes, is thinner in my sources. Ship
  Who Sang is the only mid-band source and its premise is troubling. Be careful
  not to inherit the exploitation.
- Idle fit: a house or ship has a natural tick: rooms, voyages, seasons.
  Offline time is a voyage leg or a season; the log is a captain's log.

## 4. Adjacent bodies and twists our lists miss

- **Ancillary or many-bodied mind.** One mind over many bodies, losing all but
  one (V2). Also a Culture drone swarm (V1). Our Collective row covers hives but
  not the "demoted from many to one" start.
- **Indentured or owned start.** Shell people (V3), Dorfl before his receipt
  (C6), Murderbot's governor (C1): the start is a *legal status*, and freedom is
  a purchase or hack. Add "property" as a cross-body status.
- **Made-for-a-purpose with a hidden rule.** Governor module (C1), instruction
  slip (C6), independence check (C9).
- **Contract-bound spirit powering a place.** Calcifer (V4). A hearth demon,
  a house spirit, a lantern spirit (tsukumogami).
- **House as family mirror.** Casita's cracks (V5).
- **Tsukumogami by neglect.** An object whose awakening is triggered by being
  discarded (O5). A pre-born "thrown away" start.
- **Mind downscale.** Ship into android (C10), cyborg in a prosthetic shell (C12).
- **Fragment and reassembly.** The Iron Giant's scattered pieces (C11).
- **Menu of failure endings.** NieR's 21 extra endings (C8).
- **Restart as a new AI.** Talos's "fail and restart" (C9) matches our
  reincarnation shell neatly.
- **Appearance penalty.** Frankenstein's creature (C5).
- **Heirloom with an agenda.** Stormbringer inverts the usual sword (O4).

## 5. Strongest five ideas for this cluster

1. **Object life as a chain of wielders**, with the sword's tiers of being heard
   (sentient unheard, heard by one, heard by all) as the channel progression
   (O1, O4). The bio is a list of hands.
2. **Vending machine economy as the core idle loop**: stock, price, points, upgrade
   (O2). Cleanest bridge between a body fantasy and the existing game.
3. **A hidden rule on a made body**: Murderbot's governor, the golem's slip,
   Talos's independence check (C1, C6, C9). The first big decision is whether to
   obey, hide, or edit it.
4. **Day as dice, body as debt**: Citizen Sleeper's cycle (C2) gives a
   text-friendly turn and a maintenance clock that makes idle time cost
   something.
5. **House or ship health as relationship health**: Casita's cracks (V5), with
   Howl's door dial and hidden contract clause (V4), give a family-heirs engine
   that is not just a stat.

## 6. Open questions

- Q1. Which "freedom" model do we use for made bodies: purchase (Dorfl,
  Andrew), hack (Murderbot), or defy (Talos)? Each implies a different
  mechanic.
- Q2. Is "becoming human" an ending, a path, or never offered? Sources split:
  Pinocchio and Bicentennial Man build toward it; Murderbot and Talos do not.
- Q3. How strong should a start be before the "body constraint" fades? The two
  light novels I read about give near-human powers early; we may want slower.
- Q4. Do we adopt the exploitation of Ship Who Sang's premise as a theme or
  avoid it? I lean avoid, but it is a design choice for Weibao.
- Q5. Fated Villain's ring spirit and the exact clone systems of Legendary
  Mechanic remain unverified. A chapter-level read would settle them.
- Q6. Cars, Wall-E and Heart of Gold are named but not researched here.
